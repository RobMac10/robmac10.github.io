Walking on Ancient Ground

Time Travel on Country · A Choose Your Own Character Adventure

Three lives. One ancient Country. One moment of history, seen through three sets of eyes.

Stand with the Resistor, a warrior defending Country as the newcomers’ farms block the creeks. March beside Thomas Freeman, a First Fleet clerk who goes into the bush with Captain Watkin Tench, hunting a man he begins to suspect is no savage at all. Arrive with Thomas Curtis, a Hampshire labourer who has sailed for one hundred and thirteen days to escape the workhouse, and who meets a Gadigal man named Warra on his first evening lost in the bush.

The book is based on Tench’s journals, colonial records and family memory. It asks: Is it ever right to fight back? What does survival really cost? Can one small kindness change what comes next?

Classroom app: Billy and the Native Police

A choose-your-path story set in Central Queensland in the late 1870s. You play Billy, a young Aboriginal trooper in the Native Mounted Police. Each choice has a cost, and there are five endings. It is written for teenage readers.

Cultural content warning

This resource may contain names and images of deceased Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. It is about frontier violence. It does not show violence in detail, but it does describe it.

The app has not yet been reviewed by Traditional Owners. Teachers, please seek advice from the Traditional Owners of your area before using it in class. In Central Queensland they include, for example, the Darumbal, Gaangalu and Wulli Wulli peoples.

The history behind it

The Native Mounted Police operated in Queensland from 1849 to 1904. It was a force of Aboriginal troopers led by white officers. A detachment usually had four to twelve troopers and one European officer. The force moved north and west as settlers took up new land, and its main work was to answer settlers’ requests to “disperse” Aboriginal people. In the records, “dispersal” was a polite word for shooting and killing.

Pastoral settlement in Central Queensland grew from the late 1840s. Native Police barracks were built at Rockhampton in 1860 and on the Nogoa River in 1861. Aboriginal people resisted the loss of their country, and the Darumbal, Gangalu and neighbouring peoples were among those who fought to keep it.

Many troopers were taken from their own families and country, or had very little choice. Historians are careful about judging them. The records of the force were written almost entirely by officers and settlers, so very little is known from the troopers themselves.

What the app is

Billy and the other characters are invented. The story avoids any real massacre, and Billy does not take part in one. The story also does not describe the killing of Aboriginal people. Each scene has a “Real history” panel that gives the facts and the source. A Behind the Story page lists what is real and what is invented. A Discuss and write page has class questions.

Classroom app: The Sugar Story

A choose-your-path story about sugar, labour and law in Queensland from 1884 to 1908. You play Harry, a young man from the Pacific Islands who comes to work on a sugar plantation near Mackay. Your choices decide how you meet the changes that follow, and there are five endings. It is written for teenage readers.

Cultural content warning

This resource may contain names and images of deceased Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander and South Sea Islander people. It describes sickness, death, unfair treatment and forced removal from Australia.

The app has not yet been reviewed by Australian South Sea Islander community groups or by Traditional Owners. Teachers, please seek advice from local Australian South Sea Islander groups, such as those linked with the Queensland United Australian South Sea Islander Council (QUASSIC), and from the Traditional Owners of your area before using it in class.

The history behind it

From 1863 to 1904, Queensland records show about 62,000 indenture contracts for Pacific Islanders, who came mainly from the New Hebrides (now Vanuatu) and the Solomon Islands. Most worked on sugar plantations, and most were young men. Some people were taken by force or by trickery, which is called blackbirding. Others agreed to work under a written agreement for three years. Historians still discuss how to describe the system, and the app includes both views.

Queensland passed the Polynesian Labourers Act 1868 and the Pacific Island Labourers Act 1880, and other Acts, to set rules for recruiting ships, agreements, pay and medical care. Conditions on the plantations were often hard, and many labourers died of disease and overwork, especially in the early years. Labourers also built lives in Queensland. Some made agreements with employers, saved money, bought land, married and formed communities.

From the 1880s, many sugar farmers began to sell their cane to large central mills instead of running their own plantations and mills. The new Commonwealth Parliament passed the Pacific Island Labourers Act 1901. It limited the numbers who could enter until 1904 and allowed deportations from the end of 1906. The government also paid bounties for sugar grown with white labour. Deportations ran from late 1906 to mid-1908, and the National Archives says about 7,500 people were forced to leave. Some people were allowed to stay. They included people who had come before 1 September 1879, and, after a Royal Commission and a change to the law in 1906, people who had lived in Australia continuously since 31 December 1886, the aged and infirm, owners of freehold land, parents of children at state schools, and people married before October 1906 to a partner from a different island. The Queensland United Australian South Sea Islander Council says that today’s Australian South Sea Islander population is largely descended from people who stayed.

The sugar industry also affected Aboriginal people. Plantations and farms were built on Aboriginal country. After 1897 the Protector of Aboriginal people could control where Aboriginal people lived and worked. Some Aboriginal and Islander people formed families and friendships. The 1906 rules put people in those families at risk of deportation.

Harry and the other characters are invented. Each scene has a “Real history” panel that gives the facts and the source. A Behind the Story page lists what is real and what is invented. A Discuss and write page has class questions. Two old photographs from the State Library of Queensland are credited, and the app has no AI images.

Classroom app: Gold Rush Paths

A choose-your-path story about the Queensland gold rushes from 1858 to 1877. You follow one of three invented people: Sam, a labourer who goes to Canoona and Gympie, Lin, a Chinese miner who goes to the Palmer River, or a young Aboriginal person on the Palmer River. There are five endings. It is written for teenage readers.

Cultural content warning

This resource may contain names of deceased Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. It describes racism against Chinese miners and violence against Aboriginal people. It does not show violence in detail.

The app has not yet been reviewed by Traditional Owners. Teachers, please seek advice from the Traditional Owners of your area before using it in class. They include, for example, the Darumbal people for Canoona, Kabi Kabi (Gubbi Gubbi) custodians for Gympie, the Gudjal people for Charters Towers, and the Traditional Owners of the Palmer River country.

The history behind it

Gold was found at many places in Queensland. In 1858 about 15,000 people are estimated to have gone to Canoona, near Rockhampton, but the field could not support them and most went back south. In 1866 Queensland was in a recession and the government offered a £3,000 reward for a new goldfield. James Nash found gold near the Mary River in 1867, and the town that grew there became Gympie. Gold was found at Charters Towers in December 1871. The Palmer River rush began in 1873, with Cooktown as its port.

Chinese miners were an important part of these rushes. On the Palmer River, Chinese miners became the large majority, and by 1876 there were about 17,000 Chinese and 1,400 European people on the field. Queensland passed laws in 1876 and 1877 that made Chinese and other Asian miners pay more, and put a tax on each Chinese arrival.

Every field was on the Country of Aboriginal people. On the Palmer River, gold seekers took waterholes and fighting followed. Native Police patrols broke up Aboriginal camps, and the historian Noreen Kirkman writes that almost all of the records were written by Europeans.

What the app is

Sam, Lin and the young Aboriginal person are invented. The Aboriginal character is given no name and no family group on purpose. Each scene has a “Real history” box that gives the facts and the source. A Behind the Story page lists what is invented, what is real and what I could not find. A Discuss and write page has class questions. The images are old public domain photographs from the State Library of Queensland, and the app has no AI images.

Classroom app: Grass Rush Paths

A choose-your-path story about squatters, runs and stations in Queensland from 1840 to 1902. You follow one of three invented people: Will, a shepherd on the Darling Downs, Alex, the manager of a sheep station in central Queensland, or a young Aboriginal person on the Darling Downs. There are five endings. It is written for teenage readers.

Cultural content warning

This resource may contain names of places where Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people died. It describes violence against Aboriginal people and violence against settlers. It does not show violence in detail.

The app has not yet been reviewed by Traditional Owners. Teachers, please seek advice from the Traditional Owners of your area before using it in class. They include, for example, the Giabal, Jarowair, Keinjan and Barunggam peoples for the Darling Downs, the Gayiri, Bidjara and Ghungalu peoples for the Springsure area, and the Darumbal people for Rockhampton.

The history behind it

From 1840, squatters took up runs on the grassland of the Darling Downs, which Aboriginal peoples had managed for thousands of years with a burning season. The squatters grazed sheep for wool and hired shepherds and hut keepers to watch the flocks. Archaeologists Lynley Wallis and Heather Burke describe the period from 1840 to the end of the 1850s on the Downs as almost continuous conflict, and the first Native Police camp in what became Queensland was set up in 1849.

Queensland became a separate colony in 1859. Land Acts, including the Crown Lands Alienation Act of 1868, opened some land to selectors, but the Queensland State Archives says the same Act let pastoralists secure land along creeks and rivers. On some central Queensland stations, spear grass and other problems led owners to change from sheep to cattle. At Gracemere near Rockhampton there were no sheep left by 1880. A freezing works opened at Townsville in 1892, and shearers went on strike in 1891.

The Federation Drought from 1895 to 1903 caused very large stock losses. The National Museum of Australia says it ended squatter-dominated pastoralism in New South Wales and Queensland, as banks foreclosed and leases were taken back. Aboriginal people also worked on stations. From 1897 Queensland law required an Aboriginal worker to be employed under a permit from a protector, and later rules controlled their wages.
